Six original candidate poems about permission — the power to ask and to be given.
The chosen one is marked CHOSEN. Each of the others carries a one-line note on why it lost.

--- A. The door (a concrete small moment) ---
The door was never locked.
Only shut.
I stood with my hand half-raised
for a year, rehearsing the knock,
until the wood itself grew tired
of waiting and swung open
on a room that had always been mine.
Note: a good image, but the ending is the expected one — the door opening itself is less surprising than a stone turning into a bird.

--- B. Yes is a small key (the single word "yes") ---
Yes is a small key.
It fits no lock you can see,
yet the whole house changes
when you turn it:
the stairs come down,
the windows learn your name,
and every dark room opens,
saying come in.
Note: "the windows learn your name" is lovely, but the key metaphor is familiar and the ending is soft — it says nothing new about asking.

--- C. The gate (the threshold between asking and being allowed) ---
Between the asking and the answer
there is a gate with no handle.
You push, and nothing moves.
You ask again — and the gate was never
a wall at all, only a question
standing where the road goes through.
Note: a clever turn, but it stays abstract — no concrete image to hold onto, unlike a stone in a pocket.

--- D. Take it (permission as a gift that changes the asker) ---
She said, take it — and the taking
changed my hands.
What I held was not the thing
but the being allowed.
Now every door I meet
I meet as someone
who has once been told yes.
Note: "changed my hands" is strong, but "the being allowed" is abstract phrasing and the ending is a statement rather than an image.

--- E. The stone (the courage to ask) — CHOSEN ---
For years I carried the question
in my pocket like a stone,
afraid to set it down.
One morning I said, may I —
and the stone opened in my palm:
a small bird, already flying,
already mine to let go.
Note: chosen. The most concrete image, the clearest turn (stone opens into a bird), the most original insight — being given means having something to let go, not to keep — and the best rhythm and ending. It says "may I?" aloud, so the permission theme is unmistakable.

--- F. May I? (a child asking) ---
May I? — two words,
a bridge of breath.
My daughter asks it of the rain,
of the dog, of the dark stairs,
and the world, which owes her nothing,
answers every time
as if it had been waiting to say yes.
Note: warm and concrete, and "a bridge of breath" is lovely — but "the world answers every time" is sentimental and not quite true, and the insight is thinner than the stone's.
